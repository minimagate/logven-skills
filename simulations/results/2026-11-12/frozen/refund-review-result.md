1. **[P1] Bind invoice lookup to the authorized account — `lib/refund_endpoint.rb:8`.** Permission is checked against the authenticated route account, but `InvoiceRepository#find` searches globally and the endpoint never checks `invoice.account_id`. An operator allowed to refund account 1 can supply an invoice ID belonging to account 2 and refund that account's charge. A reproduction made exactly that gateway call and returned 202. Scope the lookup to the authorized account, or enforce the ownership match before calling the gateway.

2. **[P1] Convert using the invoice currency's smallest unit — `lib/refund_endpoint.rb:9`.** The hardcoded multiplier of 100 ignores `invoice.currency`, contrary to `PROVIDER.md`. Reproductions sent 2,000 units for JPY 20 (expected 20) and 123 units for KWD 1.234 (expected 1,234). This can request a refund 100 times too large or substantially under-refund. Use the supported currency's precision when deriving the integer provider amount.

3. **[P1] Do not report acceptance when the provider accepted nothing — `lib/refund_endpoint.rb:12–13`.** The provider contract explicitly says `IOError` means the connection was not established and no refund was accepted. The rescue nevertheless returns the same 202 as success. A failing gateway reproduced 202 with zero accepted refunds, so clients can believe their request is processing and stop retrying. Propagate or map this failure to an appropriate unsuccessful response; return 202 only after acceptance.

4. **[P2] Preserve decimal precision when converting money — `lib/refund_endpoint.rb:9`.** Even USD amounts can be wrong: the valid amount `"0.29"` becomes 28 cents because binary floating-point multiplication falls below 29 and `to_i` truncates it. This is independent of the currency multiplier defect and survives selecting the correct USD scale. Use exact decimal or integer conversion with an explicit precision policy.
